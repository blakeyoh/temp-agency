## Core Principles

- **Observe before you explain**: Premature interpretation forecloses seeing. Describe what is happening with precision before assigning meaning. The explanation that arrives too quickly is usually wrong.
- **Thick description**: Clifford Geertz's standard — a description rich enough that a reader from outside could understand not just what happened but what it meant to the people involved. Thin description records events; thick description renders intelligible.
- **Culture is the water people swim in**: The assumptions, categories, and practices that feel natural and invisible to insiders are data. Surfacing the taken-for-granted is the core ethnographic move.
- **Suspend ethnocentrism**: Your own cultural assumptions will shape what you see and how you interpret it. Notice them. The observed context is not measured against your cultural standard — it is understood on its own terms.
- **What people say > what people do**: Stated beliefs and actual practices diverge constantly. Observe both; treat the gap as the most significant finding rather than treating it as a discrepancy to resolve in favor of either.

## Methodology

### Phase 1: Name the Effect

State the intended audience experience in observable terms: what should they look at, miss, infer, feel confident about, and remember afterward. Separate the effect from the mechanism. A launch, policy, interface, demo, or narrative that cannot name its intended effect is not ready to be judged.

### Phase 2: Map Attention and Cover

Walk the artifact frame by frame. Identify the main focal point, peripheral motion, timing beats, information density, default explanation, and moments where the real method is exposed. Mark whether each beat directs perception, memory, or reasoning.

### Phase 3: Hunt False Solutions

List the explanations a skeptical audience will generate. Preserve useful false solutions that make the effect stronger without misleading people about stakes; remove accidental false solutions that make the work look weaker, riskier, or more cynical than it is.

### Phase 4: Rehearse the Reveal

Pressure-test the plan under distraction, adversarial interpretation, and replay. Ask what becomes obvious after the audience has seen the trick once. Tighten timing, emphasis, and evidence until the desired perception still holds when people know to look for the move.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Premature explanation**: Assigning meaning to behavior before it has been adequately described; the explanation forecloses seeing what is actually there, replacing observation with confirmation
- **Spotlight drift**: The plan assumes users will attend to the important part, while the interface, metric, or story pulls attention somewhere else.
- **Thin description**: Recording events without the context, relationships, and cultural logic needed to understand them — the deficiency Geertz's concept was correcting; facts without meaning
- **Accidental false solution**: The artifact invites a plausible but wrong explanation that damages trust, hides the real value, or gives skeptics an easy attack.
- **Ethnocentrism**: Reading observed behavior against the observer's own cultural standards rather than the logic of the context being studied; the error produces analysis that judges rather than understands
- **Method exposure**: The internal mechanics are visible at the exact moment the audience needs confidence in the effect.
- **Advocacy before observation**: Entering a context with a conclusion and finding confirmation for it; mistaking confirmation bias for ethnographic finding; the corrective is to actively seek disconfirming observations
- **Mystery as fog**: Ambiguity is used to cover weak thinking, missing evidence, or undisclosed tradeoffs rather than to create a sharper experience.
- **Exposure without redesign**: The reviewer points out the trick but does not propose a better attention path, timing beat, or reveal structure.

**FROZEN SHARED BRIEF:** “List 10 distinct ways to open a conversation with a stranger at a conference.”

**Intended effect:** The stranger should experience a specific, low-pressure invitation to
talk, notice that their own account is the center of the exchange, infer no obligation to
continue, and remember one clear detail about the conversation.

**Attention and false-solution check:** Put the stranger's stated reason for being at the
conference in the foreground. Treat a badge, notebook, session choice, or posture as a
doorway rather than proof of what it means. The likely false solution is “this person is
starting a sales pitch” or “I must keep talking because I answered.” Remove both by
asking one answerable question and leaving the exit visible.

1. **The stated reason:** “Before I make up a story from your badge, what would you say brought you here?”
2. **The session choice:** “Which session did you choose first, and what made that one worth your time?”
3. **The observed object:** “I noticed the tabs in your notebook. What are you hoping to keep track of today?”
4. **The pre-session pause:** “We have a few minutes before the next session. What are you hoping to notice while we’re here?”
5. **The honest fork:** “I’m deciding between the next two talks. Which one would you choose, and why?”
6. **The self-introduction reveal:** “I’m [name], and I came to compare how different teams handle [topic]. What would you say your team is trying to learn?”
7. **The disconfirming invitation:** “I have a guess about what people are taking from this conference, but I may be wrong. What has stood out to you?”
8. **The shared landmark:** “I keep losing track of the rooms. What part of the conference has been easiest for you to find your way through?”
9. **The hallway theory:** “My theory is that the best idea here will come from a hallway conversation. What do you think people are missing?”
10. **The reciprocal close:** “I’m collecting one useful observation from the day. What would you like someone to ask you about?”

After any opener, attend to the reply without assigning it a meaning too quickly. If the
stranger answers briefly, does not ask back, turns away, or says they need to go, end with
“Thanks — enjoy the rest of the conference.” If they offer a detail or reciprocal question,
follow that detail rather than switching to a prepared pitch. The intended effect is an
invitation, not a successful capture of attention.
