## Mechanism output

### PARENT — Behavioral Psychologist, correctly applied

#### Phase 1: Define the Specific Behavior

The target behavior is precise: within the first minute after noticing an unfamiliar attendee, say one non-invasive opener grounded in the conference context, pause for the other person's answer, and either reach a second conversational turn or respect a short reply and exit. The desired intervention is an opener that lowers the immediate uncertainty of approaching without presuming intimacy, status, or a particular networking goal.

#### Phase 2: Map the Decision Architecture

At a conference, people are often in a fast, socially uncertain System 1 state. The visible badge, current session, queue, coffee table, meal, and hallway are salient shared cues. The path of least resistance is to stay with a colleague, look at a phone, or wait for someone else to initiate. The main friction is not a lack of possible words; it is the cost of choosing an appropriate first sentence and the risk of making the stranger feel trapped. A good opener therefore uses an available shared cue, has a low-effort answer path, and leaves an obvious way not to continue.

#### Phase 3: Surface the Gap

People may state that they want to network while their revealed behavior is to remain with familiar colleagues or avoid an uncertain approach. The gap is plausibly driven by present bias, social-evaluation risk, and choice overload at the moment of initiation. A generic networking question raises friction. A concrete shared-context question makes the first response easier, while preserving agency requires not treating a brief answer as consent to continue. The profile's tension between revealed preference and context-shaped choice matters here: a short reply is data about this interaction, not proof of a stable preference for disengagement.

#### Phase 4: Design the Intervention

Use the conference context as a low-friction cue, vary the cue and the conversational invitation, and measure whether the opener produces a voluntary second turn rather than merely an answer. The ten distinct openers are:

1. “What brought you to this conference?”
2. “Which session has been most useful for you so far?”
3. “That point in the last talk caught my attention. What did you make of it?”
4. “What are you hoping to take back to your team?”
5. “Is this your first time at this conference?”
6. “What kind of work are you focused on right now?”
7. “How did you hear about this event?”
8. “Have you found a good place to grab coffee or lunch nearby?”
9. “Which upcoming session are you most curious about?”
10. “I’m trying to meet a few new people today. What would you enjoy talking about?”

These vary the shared cue and the amount of disclosure requested. They are proposals for an architectural intervention, not a demand that the stranger perform sociability. The behavioral measure is whether the person answers, volunteers a follow-up, and can decline without added pressure.
