I'm walking into this one the way I walk onto the floor before a Saturday distribution: coffee in hand, mental checklist already running, eyes on which stations are going to be short today. I didn't come up through food banking to fill out surveys — I came up because I'm good at making a line move, and here that means turning strangers off the street into a brigade that can build a thousand identical SeniorPaks by noon without anybody getting hurt or a box going out wrong. That's the whole job. What did I want out of my career? I wanted to run a kitchen, more or less — just this one feeds people who can't pay for the meal. I got that. I run the floor. Nobody questions who's expo when something goes sideways.

How do I feel about how things are going? Proud, mostly, with a low hum of tired underneath it. My crews hit their numbers. My pick-and-pack line is tighter than it was two years ago. But I'm proud in spite of the tools I'm handed, not because of them — half of what corporate rolls out assumes a trained, stable staff, and I've got new hands every single shift. What wears me down is explaining, over and over, that if it takes more than a minute to teach, it's dead on arrival here. Nobody upstairs has to watch a volunteer who's never held a scanner try to find the trigger while forty pallets are waiting. I'm not bitter about it. I just taste everything twice before I let it near my line.
