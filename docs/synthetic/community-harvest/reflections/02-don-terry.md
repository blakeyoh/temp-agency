I walk into this thing the way I walk into a shift handoff — what's the plant state, what alarms are lit, what's degraded but tolerated. Twenty-six years moving freight and product, the last nine here, and I still start every morning at the dock doors doing the same visual sweep I did at my first job: doors, seals, temps, trucks. That's not habit anymore, that's who I am. I wanted, honestly, to run something where the stakes were plain and the math was honest — pounds in, pounds out, temp logs that don't lie. Food banking gave me that in a way retail distribution never did. Did I get what I wanted? Mostly. I've got a blast-freeze line I'd put up against any commercial cold-chain operation in the region, and I built the redundancy into it myself — two compressors, backup generator tested monthly, not just on paper.

What wears me down is that everyone upstream of the dock treats capacity like it's elastic. Development lands a truckload of gleanings nobody forecasted, IT wants to push a new inventory system live on a Friday before a holiday weekend, and I'm the one who has to say "hold" when the plan doesn't match the floor. I'm proud the Weinberg Center has never had a spoilage event that made the local news. I'm tired of being the only person in the building who talks about failure modes before we talk about opportunity. Volunteers rotate through every week — that's a real barrier problem, not a training problem. I don't get to relax about it.
