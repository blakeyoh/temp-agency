I walk into this one the way I walk into a portfolio review: looking for the deal thesis underneath the language. I've spent thirty years doing roll-ups and integrations across flow, thermal mass, and wireless networking, and what I wanted out of my career was to build a platform, not just collect logos — to own a technology roadmap that compounds instead of a shelf of unrelated acquisitions. Largely I got it. TASI Measurement is a real platform now, and Pyromation was a disciplined buy: strong margins, a defensible speed moat, an NVLAP lab nobody can fake overnight. I'm proud of that diligence and of the cross-sell thesis I authored — Pyromation sensors riding SignalFire's wireless nodes and AW-Lake's flow instrumentation into one procurement conversation instead of three.

What wears on me is the integration tax. Every acquisition I've run has the same second act: the plant culture resists the reporting structure, morale dips in the value streams that built the thing I bought, and I have to decide how much autonomy to protect versus how much discipline to impose. Fort Wayne is living that now. I don't see the shop floor day to day — Sawyer and Beckman do — so I'm honestly reconstructing my read of "an ordinary week" from board packages, capital requests, and secondhand signal. I trust the numbers on speed and quality. I'm more skeptical whenever someone brings me an initiative with enthusiasm but no documented return or cross-portfolio synergy. That's the gate I hold, and I hold it because I've seen what happens to a division when nobody does.
