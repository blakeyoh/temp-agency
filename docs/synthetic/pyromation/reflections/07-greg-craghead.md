I walk into this thing the way I walk into most internal exercises now — glad someone's asking, wary of what the answers will get compressed into by the time they reach Fort Meade or wherever TASI reads these. Twenty years in, I know the difference between what we tell a plant engineer in a catalog and what actually happens on the floor, and that gap is the thing I think about more than almost anyone else in this building, because I'm the one who has to write the sentence that papers over it.

What did I want? To build something honest that outlasted me — a body of technical literature people in this industry actually trust, not marketing copy. I got most of that. The catalogs are good. The Purdue relationship is real. I'm proud of both. What wears me down is structural, not personal: I sell through distributors, so I almost never watch a plant engineer actually touch the product, which means I'm writing from inference dressed as authority. Post-acquisition, I feel it more — TASI wants tighter numbers, faster gates, and I understand why, but Pete's old company ran on trust I could observe directly and now I'm often reporting secondhand information as if I'd witnessed it myself. I feel steady about the company, uneasy about how much of what I say I know is actually just well-informed guessing.
