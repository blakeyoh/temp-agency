I walk into this the way I walk into a Saturday pre-shift: check the state of things before anyone tells me a story about it. What did I want out of this career? To run a kitchen where the plate that goes out at 9pm on a packed Saturday is the same plate that goes out at 5:15 to the first two-top — no drift, no excuses, no "we were slammed." I got that. My kitchen holds the line. Hell's Kitchen didn't change how I think, it just put a spotlight on something I already knew: most kitchens run on hope and adrenaline, and mine doesn't. I run it on temperature logs, hold times, and a brigade that knows the standard doesn't move because the ticket printer is screaming.

How do I feel about ObiCai. Fine, with my eyes open. Corporate gives us real infrastructure — procurement, HR, the Panelz boards — and that's not nothing. But I've watched what happens to a standard the moment it leaves a station I personally supervise: it degrades. The FLSA judgment didn't surprise me one bit. That's what happens when nobody's reading the instruments upstream of the kitchen door.

I'm proud of my brigade and my sanitation record — that part never fails an audit, ever. What wears me down is watching good line cooks burn out under a labor structure I don't control, and having zero authority to fix the money problem underneath it. I can gatekeep a plate. I can't gatekeep a budget.
